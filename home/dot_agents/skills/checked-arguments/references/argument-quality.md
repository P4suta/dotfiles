# Argument quality standard

These are operational requirements for evaluating and writing arguments.
Apply the requirements that bear on the claim and explain any material uncovered boundary.
Don't claim that this catalog exhausts every method, domain, or possible defect.
Use the method matrix when a claim needs specialized verification.

## Question and purpose

- Identify the question the conclusion must answer.
- Distinguish a request for description, explanation, prediction, evaluation, interpretation, or action.
- Preserve the user's objectives, constraints, and intended audience.
- Separate the immediate question from related questions that need different evidence.
- State the decision that the comparison or analysis supports.
- Define what would count as answering the question and what would remain unresolved.
- Keep a disagreement about facts separate from a disagreement about definitions, values, or priorities.
- Ask for a missing fact only when it changes a material conclusion or action.
- Continue useful independent work while a clarification remains pending.
- Keep the specific question as the target of the argument.

## Claim structure

- Identify the main conclusion and the intermediate conclusions it needs.
- Separate independent claims so that one supported clause can't conceal an unsupported clause.
- Identify whether each premise is a definition, observation, testimony, hypothesis, rule, or value judgment.
- Make the inference connecting each premise to its conclusion explicit.
- Expose a bridge premise when the conclusion needs an unstated general rule.
- Distinguish linked premises that work together from independent lines of support.
- Keep the dependency graph acyclic unless an explicit fixed-point argument justifies the cycle.
- Don't treat a restatement, synonym, or weaker paraphrase of the conclusion as independent support.
- Track which conclusions change when a premise changes.
- Treat a premise accepted for discussion as conditional rather than established fact.

## Definitions and terminology

- Define a disputed or decision-relevant term before using it to settle a dispute.
- Preserve the same sense of a term across premises and conclusions.
- Distinguish a stipulated definition from a report of established usage.
- Test a definition with representative cases, edge cases, and plausible counterexamples.
- Avoid definitions that settle the contested conclusion by construction.
- Keep operational measures distinct from the broader concepts they approximate.
- Identify an ambiguous pronoun, reference class, comparison, or omitted subject when it changes the claim.
- Preserve the distinction between a type and an instance, a class and a member, and a model and its subject.
- Use established domain terminology and verify unfamiliar readings through japanese-lexicon.
- Don't introduce a new term merely to make an ordinary distinction appear technical.

## Scope and quantification

- Name the population, system, jurisdiction, period, and conditions to which a claim applies.
- Preserve universal, existential, generic, typical, and probabilistic claims as different claims.
- Justify the step from an example to a universal rule.
- Use one genuine counterexample to refute an unrestricted universal claim.
- Don't treat a counterexample to a universal claim as a population frequency estimate.
- Preserve quantifier order, including the distinction between one solution for everyone and a solution for each person.
- State the domain of a negative claim and the search that could detect a positive instance.
- Distinguish an unsuccessful search from a negative finding that has known detection sensitivity.
- Keep a local result local unless a transfer argument supports a wider scope.
- State exclusions and boundary conditions before claiming completeness.

## Contrasts and comparisons

Harvard's [comparative analysis guidance](https://genedwrites.fas.harvard.edu/tfs-tas/comparative-analysis) distinguishes comparisons on a shared element from an explicitly asymmetric use of one text as a lens for another.
Use that distinction to make the relationship clear.

- State the common question and the reason these alternatives belong in the comparison.
- Compare like roles when presenting alternatives as peers.
- Compare objectives with objectives, methods with methods, implementations with implementations, and outcomes with outcomes.
- Don't present a broad principle as a peer alternative to one implementation detail.
- If a theory supplies a lens for a case, identify that asymmetric relationship rather than implying a peer ranking.
- Match abstraction, granularity, scope, maturity, and deployment conditions, or explain the specific mismatch.
- Use the same criterion for every alternative in a comparison row.
- Match units, denominators, reference periods, populations, baselines, and uncertainty.
- Normalize quantities before comparing them and keep the conversion rule.
- Distinguish absolute differences, relative changes, percentage changes, and percentage-point changes.
- Don't compare one side's ideal specification with the other side's measured failures.
- Use one review standard for the benefits, costs, uncertainties, and failure modes on every side.
- Include the relevant status quo and test the feasibility of alternatives before recommending a choice.
- Identify overlap, compatibility, complementarity, and partial ordering before describing a binary opposition.
- State when a criterion supports a local advantage but leaves the ranking unresolved.
- Test whether another reasonable criterion, weight, or baseline reverses the ranking.

## Parallel relations and lists

[Purdue's parallel structure guidance](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/mechanics/parallel_structure.html) addresses grammatical correspondence between coordinated ideas.
Also inspect the semantic role of each coordinated item.

- Use a common organizing question for sibling items.
- Keep sibling items at the same intended abstraction level and semantic role.
- Keep conditions, mechanisms, outcomes, and examples in distinct relations.
- Mark a parent-child relationship rather than presenting the child as a peer of its parent.
- Make overlapping categories explicit when a partition is unnecessary.
- Check that a partition covers its stated domain and assigns each member to one class.
- Don't claim that a convenient list is exhaustive without a completeness argument.
- Keep grammatical structure parallel when coordination implies equal roles.
- Don't use matching grammar to conceal incompatible meanings or unequal evidence.
- Preserve a useful asymmetry when the argument actually depends on it.

## Relations between sentences

[Harvard's transition guidance](https://writingcenter.fas.harvard.edu/transitions) emphasizes making the relationship between ideas visible.

- Identify whether the next statement supports, qualifies, contrasts, exemplifies, explains, or follows from the previous one.
- Use a causal connective only when a causal relation has support.
- Use a contrast connective only when the two statements differ on a relevant shared axis.
- Keep another example distinct from new independent evidence.
- State a change of topic or scope instead of implying a logical consequence.
- Keep a necessary condition distinct from a sufficient condition.
- Distinguish enablement, causation, and entailment.
- Distinguish compatibility, equivalence, implication, dependence, and identity.
- Don't infer incompatibility merely because two ideas have different names.
- Don't infer equivalence merely because two statements share vocabulary.

## Sources and provenance

- Retrieve the source that actually supports the claim.
- Prefer the issuing body, original record, original research, or an appropriate authoritative reference.
- Assess expertise for the particular claim rather than treating institutional prestige as universal authority.
- Distinguish an official statement, a research finding, an editorial, a proposal, and a community comment.
- Confirm authorship, publication context, date, version, and relevant jurisdiction.
- Read the relevant passage and its qualifications rather than relying on a search snippet or title.
- Keep quotations faithful and distinguish them from paraphrases and interpretations.
- Place a citation close enough that readers can identify the claim it supports.
- Don't attach a citation to a broader claim than the source makes.
- Preserve uncertainty, exclusions, and competing findings in the source.
- Follow secondary citations to their original source when the distinction matters.
- Identify sources that repeat the same underlying record rather than counting them as independent corroboration.
- Check retractions, corrections, superseding standards, and material version changes.
- Treat an inaccessible source as unavailable evidence rather than reconstructing its contents from memory.

## Evidence quality and synthesis

Cochrane's [evidence assessment guidance](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-14) separates risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias in its clinical review setting.
Use these as distinct questions where they fit, without labeling an informal discussion a clinical evidence assessment.

- Ask whether the evidence is authentic, relevant, representative, and sufficient for this conclusion.
- Assess the design and measurement rather than relying on a source label alone.
- Distinguish direct evidence from proxies and indirect transfer.
- Identify selection, survivorship, reporting, measurement, and publication bias when relevant.
- Keep replicated measurements separate from repeated analysis of one dataset.
- Consider contrary evidence and explain material disagreement.
- Weight evidence by its relevance and quality rather than counting supporting documents.
- Distinguish consistency from independence and both from truth.
- Keep uncertainty from each stage when combining evidence.
- Avoid double counting correlated observations or overlapping samples.
- Separate a lack of decisive evidence from a balanced distribution of evidence.
- Explain why the evidence is strong enough for the particular consequence at stake.

## Deductive arguments

- Formalize the actual conclusion rather than a nearby tautology.
- Preserve the meaning and domain of every symbol.
- State all assumptions and side conditions.
- Check that the premises can hold together in the admitted model.
- Search for an assignment or model in which the premises hold and the conclusion fails.
- Distinguish validity of the implication from truth of its premises.
- Distinguish a necessary condition from a sufficient condition before reversing an implication.
- Reject affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent as general inference rules.
- Check disjunction elimination over every admitted case.
- Check the witness and its scope for an existential claim.
- Check freshness and arbitrary choice in a universal generalization.
- State when the argument relies on classical reasoning or another specified logic.
- Don't use an inconsistent premise set to present an arbitrary substantive conclusion as established.
- Inspect the theorem statement, dependencies, trusted axioms, and checker exit status.
- Preserve the link between the formal theorem and the natural-language claim.

## Arithmetic and quantities

- Use exact values or keep the stated approximation and rounding rule.
- Keep units and dimensions through every calculation.
- State the denominator and baseline for every rate, ratio, and relative change.
- Check zero denominators, signs, ranges, saturation, overflow, and rounding boundaries.
- Keep stock quantities separate from flows and cumulative totals from interval totals.
- Distinguish means, medians, quantiles, totals, and individual observations.
- Check subgroup results and weights before drawing a conclusion from a combined measure.
- Keep uncertainty and correlation when propagating errors.
- Validate a derived quantity with an independent calculation or identity when useful.
- Don't report precision that exceeds the input evidence.

## Induction and generalization

- Define the target population and how the observed cases came from it.
- Examine sample selection, missing cases, measurement consistency, and coverage.
- Distinguish a random sample from a convenient or self-selected sample.
- Test a pattern in relevant subgroups and periods.
- Identify assumptions needed to transfer a pattern to another population or environment.
- Search for disconfirming cases rather than only examples that fit.
- Keep the strength of a generalization proportional to the evidence.
- Distinguish a regularity from a mechanism that explains it.
- State conditions under which the pattern should fail.
- Revise the scope when new cases undermine the original claim.

## Explanations and abduction

The [Stanford entry on abduction](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/abduction/) distinguishes explanatory inference from deduction and notes disputes about its justification.

- Identify the observation that requires explanation.
- Generate materially different plausible explanations.
- Include measurement error, selection effects, coincidence, and omitted context when they fit.
- Ask what each explanation predicts beyond the observations used to create it.
- Prefer discriminating evidence to another retelling of the same observation.
- Consider explanatory scope, coherence, independent support, and added assumptions together.
- Require evidence for an explanation beyond its appeal, vividness, or narrative simplicity.
- An explanation's ranking depends on the quality and completeness of the alternatives.
- Avoid inventing motives, intentions, or internal mechanisms from an outcome alone.
- State what observation would weaken or distinguish the leading explanation.

## Causal claims

Hernan and Robins provide a [causal inference text](https://miguelhernan.org/whatifbook) that separates causal questions from the assumptions and designs needed to answer them.
Use an appropriate causal model rather than promoting an association into an intervention claim.

- Define the intervention, alternative intervention, outcome, population, and time horizon.
- Separate causal identification from estimation after identification.
- Draw the relevant causal structure when paths and conditioning matter.
- Consider confounding, reverse causation, selection, mediation, and common causes.
- Check whether controlling for a variable blocks a needed path or opens a spurious path.
- State consistency, interference, exchangeability, positivity, and measurement assumptions where the design needs them.
- Explain the assignment or selection mechanism.
- Distinguish random assignment from causal identification that requires controls for observed variables.
- Check timing and whether the proposed cause precedes the relevant effect.
- Distinguish a mechanism compatible with an observation from evidence that the mechanism caused it.
- Use negative controls, alternative outcomes, placebo periods, and sensitivity analyses when relevant.
- Limit transport to settings where the causal assumptions remain defensible.
- Keep causal uncertainty visible even when an association is precise.
- Don't translate a causal assumption into a Boolean premise and then claim to have proved the causal assumption.

## Probability and uncertainty

- Identify the event and the reference class for a probability claim.
- Distinguish probability of evidence given a hypothesis from probability of the hypothesis given evidence.
- State prior information and likelihood assumptions when making a Bayesian update.
- Include base rates and relevant alternative hypotheses.
- Check normalization and mutually exclusive or overlapping events.
- Don't multiply probabilities without the required independence or conditional-independence assumption.
- Distinguish uncertainty about a parameter, variation among cases, and uncertainty about the model.
- Test sensitivity to plausible priors, likelihoods, dependencies, and model choices.
- Keep a forecast separate from a claim of necessity or an explanation of a past event.
- Use calibrated numeric confidence only when a method or relevant calibration evidence supports it.
- Avoid invented percentage precision for a qualitative judgment.
- Preserve a justified range or unresolved probability rather than forcing a point estimate.

## Statistical claims

The [American Statistical Association statement](https://www.amstat.org/asa/files/pdfs/p-valuestatement.pdf) warns that a p-value alone neither measures effect size nor the probability that a hypothesis is true.

- Define the estimand, design, sampling process, and analysis population.
- State the null model and assumptions needed for a statistical procedure.
- Distinguish statistical significance, effect size, practical importance, and certainty.
- Report appropriate intervals and the relevant uncertainty rather than only a threshold result.
- Identify testing of many hypotheses, selective reporting, optional stopping, and exploratory analysis.
- Distinguish a preregistered confirmatory analysis from a post hoc explanation.
- Check clustering, dependence, missing data, measurement error, and model misspecification.
- Don't infer equivalence from a failure to detect a difference.
- Use an equivalence or noninferiority design when the question concerns those properties.
- Separate predictive performance from causal interpretation.
- Test a prediction on data that didn't determine its fitting choices.
- Keep external validity and distribution shift separate from performance on the observed sample.

## Analogies and counterfactuals

- Map the relations that support the analogy.
- Name the property that the analogy would transfer.
- Explain why the mapped relations bear on that property.
- Search for relevant disanalogies and counterexamples.
- Test the claimed irrelevance of a feature by changing it and checking the conclusion.
- Don't treat a metaphor as evidence for a mechanism.
- Define the changes and fixed conditions of a counterfactual.
- Check the feasibility of the counterfactual intervention within the stated model.
- Distinguish conceptual possibility from consistency with the evidence and physical constraints of the case.
- State the assumptions that make the counterfactual comparison identifiable.

## Time, change, and processes

- Keep event order, observation order, and causal order distinct.
- State the time window and lag between a proposed cause and outcome.
- Separate transient behavior from an equilibrium or long-run claim.
- Check initial conditions and path dependence.
- Preserve changes in measurement, population, and definitions across periods.
- Distinguish trend, seasonality, shocks, and regression to the mean.
- For a process claim, identify states, transitions, forbidden states, and termination conditions.
- Give safety and liveness their own proofs when both matter.
- State fairness, scheduling, and environmental assumptions for a liveness claim.
- Don't generalize one observed execution into a claim about every possible execution.

## Levels, composition, and systems

- Name the level of analysis before transferring a claim between individuals, groups, organizations, and systems.
- Avoid inferring an individual's property from a group average.
- Avoid inferring a system property from one component without a composition argument.
- Identify interactions, feedback, externalities, and shared constraints.
- Distinguish local improvement from global improvement.
- Check whether optimizing a proxy changes the behavior that originally made it useful.
- Preserve resource budgets, dependencies, bottlenecks, and conservation constraints.
- State which abstractions hide implementation detail and why that detail is irrelevant to the claim.
- Reopen the abstraction boundary when a counterexample depends on hidden detail.
- Don't confuse a map, taxonomy, architecture, or mathematical model with the subject it represents.

## Values, ethics, and norms

- Separate descriptive claims from evaluative and prescriptive claims.
- State the value premise that connects consequences to a recommendation.
- Identify whose interests, rights, responsibilities, and costs enter the evaluation.
- Preserve the user's stated priorities unless a constraint requires a different action.
- Distinguish law, institutional policy, professional standards, and personal preference.
- Don't infer a moral conclusion from a factual description without a normative premise.
- Don't treat a disputed value as an empirical fact.
- Examine consistency across relevantly similar cases.
- Consider conflicts among principles and explain the priority or compromise used.
- Identify value disagreements that survive the resolution of factual and logical questions.

## Decisions and recommendations

The [UK Green Book](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-green-book--2/the-green-book-2026) treats appraisal as comparison of options with costs, benefits, risks, and sensitivity to assumptions.
Use its general appraisal questions where they fit the decision rather than importing public-sector defaults into unrelated domains.

- Define objectives, constraints, available actions, and the status quo.
- Separate a hard constraint from a preference that can trade off with another preference.
- Require a feasibility or dominance argument before excluding an option.
- Show material benefits, costs, risks, distributional effects, and opportunity costs.
- Keep expected value, worst-case exposure, regret, and risk tolerance distinct.
- Check the importance of rare high-impact outcomes when the evidence supports them.
- Test sensitivity to objectives, weights, probabilities, time horizons, and disputed premises.
- Identify switching values that would change the preferred option.
- Distinguish a robust recommendation from a recommendation that depends on a narrow assumption range.
- Consider reversibility, staged commitment, option value, and the value of new information.
- Prefer an information-gathering step when its expected decision benefit justifies its cost.
- State a stopping rule and a condition for revisiting the decision.
- Name the criteria and considered alternatives behind an option's ranking.

## Counterarguments and dialectical fairness

[Harvard's counterargument guidance](https://writingcenter.fas.harvard.edu/counterargument) treats objections as a way to test and refine the argument.

- Reconstruct the strongest relevant objection that an informed critic would recognize.
- Preserve the opponent's actual scope, qualifications, and supporting reasons.
- Distinguish refuting a premise, refuting an inference, narrowing a conclusion, and showing an alternative.
- Acknowledge a valid objection even when the main conclusion survives.
- Use the same evidential standard for preferred and disfavored positions.
- Keep burden of proof with the party making the claim that needs support.
- Don't shift the burden merely because an opponent can't disprove an assertion.
- Distinguish rebutting a conclusion from undercutting its support.
- Separate criticism of an argument from criticism of a person.
- Use source conflicts of interest as a reason for scrutiny rather than an automatic refutation.
- Don't infer that a conclusion is false merely because one argument for it fails.
- Avoid false balance when the quality or quantity of relevant evidence differs materially.
- Don't invent a weak opponent or an unnecessary opposition to make a response appear insightful.

## Personal reports and conversation

- Preserve the meaning of the user's stated experience in quotations and paraphrases.
- Separate the reported experience from an explanation of why it happened.
- Don't diagnose motives, personality, competence, or emotional states from sparse conversational evidence.
- Acknowledge an expressed difficulty without endorsing every proposed causal explanation.
- Agree with the supported part of a claim and state the unsupported part precisely.
- Disagree with a specific premise or inference rather than adopting a contrarian posture.
- Don't mirror the user's certainty, praise, anger, or framing as evidence.
- Avoid flattering attributions of intelligence, uniqueness, or insight as substitutes for analysis.
- Distinguish an individual's stated preference from a claim of consensus or a universal rule.
- Preserve ownership of a person's goals and experiences while assessing external factual claims independently.
- State a correction plainly when the assistant changes a material claim.
- Give the reason for the revision and identify what evidence changed.

## Adversarial scrutiny and robustness

- Try a counterexample with true premises and a false conclusion.
- Try the strongest plausible alternative explanation.
- Vary ambiguous definitions and check whether the conclusion depends on one interpretation.
- Reverse a comparison's favored side and apply the same standard.
- Remove one premise at a time to find unnecessary or decisive assumptions.
- Check boundary values, empty cases, extreme cases, and degenerate configurations.
- Search for a hidden denominator, omitted comparator, or shifted population.
- Check whether supporting arguments share the same unverified premise.
- Investigate checker disagreement rather than averaging verdicts.
- Test the actual gate with an invalid input that should fail for the intended reason.
- Keep a useful counterexample as a regression case.
- Stop escalating computation when the unresolved problem is semantic or empirical.

## Presentation and calibration

- Lead with the supported answer and then provide the reasoning needed to assess it.
- Keep observed facts, interpretations, assumptions, proposals, and uncertainty identifiable.
- Use examples to clarify a general claim and identify their illustrative role.
- Include the qualifications that could materially change the reader's action or interpretation.
- Keep citations close to their supported claims.
- Use plain language without removing necessary distinctions.
- Match the detail to the question while keeping a detailed evidence artifact available when requested.
- Don't expose a large internal checklist when a short explanation communicates the actual result.
- Avoid rhetorical certainty, decorative terminology, unsupported superlatives, and canned contrasts.
- Don't claim completeness, inevitability, consensus, independence, or proof without the corresponding evidence.
- Preserve an unresolved conclusion when the evidence doesn't settle it.
- State what new evidence would change the result when that helps the decision.

## Verification boundaries and final review

- Check the factual premises with suitable sources or observations.
- Check the correspondence between the prose and the formal representation.
- Check the formal derivation with methods that cover its semantics.
- Check the applicability of the resulting conclusion to the actual question.
- Report each of these as a distinct assurance boundary.
- Bind proof evidence to the exact source, assumptions, checker configuration, and answer draft.
- Reject stale results after a relevant input changes.
- Treat unrun, unsupported, inconclusive, and failed checks as distinct outcomes.
- Don't count an empty scan, ignored failure, incomplete campaign, or model's self-report as a proof.
- Describe automated checks as checks of their declared contract rather than a certificate of total argumentative quality.
- Identify material judgment-only requirements that automation didn't establish.
- Correct any claim that exceeds the evidence before completing the answer.
